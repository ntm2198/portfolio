Reading Reflection (taken from slack)

With a class coming in from so many different disciplines, some technical, some educational, and some on the UX/Design side of things, I enjoyed that the 3 readings for this week laid a framework to connect these disciplines, undertanding pedagogical theory (Behaviorism, Cognitivism, Constructivism) as the framework to which we can apply design thinking (What is design knowledge and how do we teach it?) to tackle the "wicked problem" of education through the medium of tangible tools (Tangibles and Tangible Learning). Its a tall task to be interdisiplinary in a way that pushes us out of our comfort zone, especially when many of our careers are focused on the theorical "behind the scenes" aspects of education, tech, edTech, design, etc. I've recently been grappling with the disconnect between the work done on edTech platforms by designers, researchers, etc and seeing on the ground teachers really struggle to adopt or effectively use these tools while under pressure to stay trained on effective classroom management, lesson planning, working with school administrations, etc. (this is at a primary school level) Its a tension I hope we can dive in to as the class goes on! Nameley, how do we create tangibles that are easy for teachers to teach with and learners to learn from?

Week 3

Reading Reflection

For this week’s reflection I’d like to analyze an interaction I had in my STEM afterschool yesterday in terms of the reading on Antle & Wise, 2013. In this class we use LittleBits, color coded miniature circuits which use magnets & a interlocking design to make simple electronics activities more accessible. As is often the case when a new semester starts, some of my new 2nd & 3rd grade students were completely baffled by the chaotic and noisy afterschool setup and “veteran” 3rd & 4th grade lower schoolers didn’t have much patience for a direct lesson after a full day of proper school. This time around, however I noticed right off the bat younger students were interested in learning how to build simple electronic music circuits (see: https://littlebits.com/bit-o-pedia/keyboard). Importantly, I never provided direct instructions on assembling the piano, I’d shown a few people how to use the “oscillator bit” (which makes sound waves from electronic input) last year and the knowledge that such a thing was possible has stuck around. Without realizing it my students became a stunning example of “learning as a collaborative process of meaning-making and becoming a participant in the knowledge practices of a community.” (p 6)
Unfortunately, the one part of this unintentional collaborative & embodied activity that has yet to stick with my students is the actual **collaboration**, having to wait their turn for my help (there’s one of me and ~14 of them), share the littlebits, and ignore when someone else’s project (or another student themselves) was making an “annoying noise.” The “piano” is a complicated setup, it goes battery → piano → oscillator → speaker and if *any* of those parents aren’t working (LittleBits can break or be incompatible with each other) students can, in clinical terms, freak the f*ck out, blaming themselves for something not working or their fellow students for “stealing parts.” As the semester goes on, I hope to see how I can use theoretical concepts discussed in this class to better structure my class!

Week 4

Reading Reflection

For this week’s readings I wanted to highlight 2 quotes that jumped out at me as potentially being in conversation with each other and serving as a good jumping off point for our class discussion tomorrow. First in *Race after technology* “Even though they themselves were guided by a solidaristic relationship with the workers, the public granted them, as “designers,” a higher status than to their laboring counterparts.” (Benjamin, p 118) second from *Why Tangibility Matters* where after reporting positive feedback from child participants the authors note “we interpret this cautiously due to the novelty effect and researcher-child power difference.” (p. 1812) While our educational design research isn’t explicitly intended to invoke the same power imbalances that low-income workers face its worth considering how children increasingly face surveillance and tracking not dissimilar from the algorithms & systems monitoring Amazon Mechanical Turk workers (an online workforce not to be confused with warehouse & delivery workers). If this sounds a little “tinfoil hat” unfortunately the reporting on the subject suggests some massive ongoing risks to child privacy, especially when it comes to chromebooks & google classroom monitoring Read more here.



As something of a student-designer-researcher-teacher-gig worker hybrid right now its not lost on me that I hold a certain amount of authority/power over my students and am obligated as part of my job to monitor that relationship closely. For instance, I would be disinclined to test a tangible for this class with the students I teach because I might be seen by them as someone who could get them “in trouble” with the school or their parents therefore skewing the data I could get on the efficacy of a tangible learning intervention. Generally though, I’ve found doing academic work to be a positive supplement to my part-time teaching jobs in that it lets me make better decisions on how I’m using tech in the classroom and am confident in our class’s ability to have these sorts of critical discussions!
